Pet, a case study

Pet is a Tamagotchi for kids: the pet lives on a server, the robot is its body. Kids play with the robot itself and with a picture page on a phone or tablet; parents set the daily routine on a page of their own. Its code: {{.Repo}} at {{.Tag}} (MIT), online at pet.q41.eu.

What the robot and the phone show

  • The face and the LEDs follow the pet's mood, from three needs (food, fun, energy) that drop while it is awake; the LEDs glow dim in the mood's colour, and a hungry pet breathes orange.
  • Touch the head: a tickle, a cuddle, a long cuddle or scratching, each with its own line and sound. Hold an NFC food card to it: the food glides into the mouth and goes bite by bite. Shake it: "Wheee!". Come close: it says hello now and then.
  • Tap the screen: a menu of picture tiles over the face (food, play, nap, needs) with a back arrow.
  • Games: catch the ball (five balls in the screen's quarters; the head circles, wanders, then dodges a hand the light sensor sees coming); the color game (the LED strips show colours, the kid presses them in order on the screen; the three best get a photo from the robot's camera on a podium).
  • The day: a lullaby and a warm night light that fades at bedtime, a greeting and a tune in the morning, rest during school hours. It speaks its lines, with a speech bubble.
  • The phone: the kid's page works with pictures, no reading needed: the pet's face, three need bars, four big buttons. The parent's page (signed in as the robot's owner, or a PIN at home) has the routine, the food cards, the games and a log. Both are in its readme, with pictures.

How it is built

About 6,900 lines of Go and 930 of pages. Of the kit it uses robothub for the robots' connections, sound for its sounds (the pet's chimes moved into the kit), and leds for its LED colours.

The key code

The robots, through the kit's hub

As in Focus, plus two callbacks: the robot's readings every two seconds (the head's angles, motion) and its binary streams (the light sensor, a camera photo). The pet wraps the hub's robot in its own type (robotConn) for its rules and its counters.

The head stays still in a kid's hands

From the motion readings the pet learns the robot's rest pose, and calls it held when it tilts away from it, turns or is shaken (held.go). While held, every head command is dropped, in one place: the robot's connection.

{{template "excerpt" (excerpt "pet/held")}}

Its pictures and sounds, kept on the robot

At connect the pet asks for the robot's file list, keeps what is there with the right checksum (CRC-32), deletes its old files and uploads the rest in chunks, paced behind the commands. Later it shows a picture by its name: a few bytes instead of a JPEG.

A menu from tappable sprites

Each tile is a stored picture shown as a sprite marked tap; the robot's tap event names the sprite it hit, so the pet needs no hit test. The menu closes by itself after 15 s.

Timed steps that a newer reaction cancels

Reactions are short scenes: steps at times. Each reaction bumps a counter; a step whose counter is old is dropped, so a quick second touch never mixes two scenes.

A scene: the food glides into the mouth

A sprite drops in from above the screen, glides down in 700 ms (the robot animates it), and shrinks bite by bite with a munch and a nod.

A hand seen by the light sensor

During catch the ball the robot streams its light and proximity sensor 20 times a second. A hand coming close raises the proximity or darkens the light; the baselines follow the room. The stream is on only during a game.

What it teaches

  • Make meaning from raw input. The robot reports presses with their zones, releases with their length, swipes and tags; the pet decides what was a tickle, a cuddle or scratching, and waits half a second so a food card wins over the hand that brought it. Dev's gesture package does the general part (a pat, a pet, a stroke) for every program.
  • Guard the body. A robot in a kid's hands must not twist its head: one check where commands leave the app covers every reaction and game.
  • Keep pictures on the robot. Upload once, by checksum; show by name. Sprites move, fade and scale on the robot itself, and a tappable sprite is a button.
  • Give timed steps a generation. A counter cancels the rest of a scene when a newer one starts; no timers to track down.
  • Turn streams on only while needed. The light stream runs during a game; at connect the pet turns it off, in case a game was cut short.
  • Fall back. Without sprites, whole pictures; without the light stream, the head dodges on a timer; without a stored sound, the sound itself.
  • Repair what got lost. A command can be dropped (a full queue) or undone (the robot blanks its screen after a minute without commands); a watchdog notices and shows the pet's face again.
  • A pure model, an app test with a fake robot. The needs and the schedule are tested with times passed in; the app test drives a fake robot and a settable clock through the games, bedtime and the morning.
